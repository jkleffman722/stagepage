# Tour Production Management — Domain Reference

This document covers the full operational context of live music touring production management. It is used to inform product decisions for StagePage and to ground the Tour PM research agent in real-world workflow.

---

## The People Involved

### On the Tour

**Tour Production Manager (Tour PM)**
The operational center of the production. Responsible for everything that happens onstage and backstage — budget, logistics, crew, equipment, advancing, schedule, and settlement. They interface with every department and every venue. On larger tours, this is a full-time role on the road 200+ days a year. On smaller tours, the Tour Manager may double as the PM.

**Tour Manager (TM)**
Manages the artist's day-to-day life on the road — travel, hotel, per diems, itineraries, merchandise settlements, and artist relations. The TM and PM work closely but have distinct domains: TM owns the artist's world, PM owns the production world.

**Production Coordinator**
Assists the PM with logistics, document management, vendor coordination, and communication. Common on larger tours. Often the person maintaining the advance sheets and sending emails.

**FOH Engineer (Front of House)**
Mixes the live show from the FOH position (audience-facing, usually at a desk in the middle of the crowd). Carries strong preferences about console model, I/O format, and PA systems. One of the first departments to need confirmed advance info.

**Monitor Engineer**
Mixes the stage monitors and/or IEM (in-ear monitor) mixes for the performers. Works at the side of the stage. Closely tied to the input list and monitor world specs.

**Lighting Director (LD)**
Designs and operates the lighting show. Has strong preferences about console brand, DMX universe count, available fixtures, and follow spot positions. Needs trim height and rigging capacity confirmed early.

**Stage Manager / Head Carpenter**
Manages the stage deck: the physical build of the stage set, props, soft goods, staging elements. Needs stage dimensions, surface type, and wing space confirmed well in advance.

**Head Rigger**
Oversees all rigging — hanging truss, motors, PA hangs, lighting rigs. Needs rigging point count, weight capacity per point, and trim height. Critical for safety compliance.

**Backline Technicians**
Guitar tech, drum tech, keyboard tech. Each manages a specific instrument area. Usually only care about power locations and stage space.

**Merchandise Manager**
Handles merch sales and settlement. Needs confirmed merch location, square footage, electrical, and settlement contact at the venue.

**Pyro / SFX Tech**
Manages special effects (pyrotechnics, CO2, confetti, etc.). Requires advance approval from venue and sometimes local fire marshal. Not present on every tour.

### At the Venue

**Venue Production Manager (Venue PM)**
The Tour PM's primary counterpart. Knows the building inside out — where the power panels are, which dock the trucks use, how long load-in actually takes, what the crew is like. The quality of the advance hinges entirely on this relationship and their responsiveness.

**Venue General Manager (GM)**
Overall operations of the venue. Usually involved in share request approvals and high-level logistics. Less involved in day-to-day technical advancing.

**House Sound Engineer**
The venue's resident FOH engineer. May or may not be used by touring acts — many tours carry their own. If used, they know the room and the PA system intimately.

**House Lighting Designer**
Venue's resident LD. Same dynamic as house sound — touring LDs often prefer to use their own system or operate the house rig themselves.

**Head Carpenter / Stage Manager**
Venue-side counterpart to the tour's stage manager. Manages the local stagehand crew and knows the stage.

**Local Crew (Stagehands)**
Hired for each show through the local IATSE chapter (union) or a non-union labor company. Quality varies significantly. The Tour PM sets crew call times and manages this crew through the Venue PM and local department heads.

**Promoter**
The company or individual who bought the show — Goldenvoice, Live Nation, AEG Presents, or an independent. They handle ticketing, marketing, and the financial settlement at the end of the night. The Tour Manager or PM interacts with the promoter rep on settlement.

**Box Office Manager**
Manages ticketing and comps. Relevant for guest list, settlement, and any walk-up ticket issues.

## The Advancing Process — Step by Step

### Phase 1: Pre-Production (Weeks to Months Out)

The tour is booked. The booking agent has confirmed dates, venues, and deal structures with promoters. The Tour PM receives a routing sheet — a list of all shows with dates, venues, and deals.

The PM begins building the tour bible: a master document containing every show, every contact, every piece of logistics. Each venue needs to be advanced individually.

Documents the PM has ready before advancing begins:
- **Technical Rider** — the artist's production requirements (what the tour needs from the venue)
- **Hospitality Rider** — dressing room requirements, catering, specific artist requests
- **Stage Plot** — diagram showing where every musician and piece of equipment sits onstage
- **Input List** — channel-by-channel list of every audio input
- **Lighting Plot** (sometimes) — diagram of the lighting rig the tour is carrying

### Phase 2: Initial Advance (4–8 Weeks Out)

The PM sends an initial advance email to the Venue PM:
- Introduces themselves and the tour
- Attaches the technical rider and any other relevant documents
- Requests the venue's tech packet / technical specifications
- Asks for confirmation of load-in time, crew call, and settlement contact

The venue responds with their tech packet — often a PDF that may or may not be current.

The PM begins filling in their advance sheet template for this show, using the venue packet as a source.

### Phase 3: Technical Advance (3–5 Weeks Out)

The PM reviews the venue specs against the rider and identifies:
- **Hard conflicts** — things that must be resolved (e.g., venue has 100A service, tour needs 200A)
- **Soft conflicts** — things that need clarification (e.g., "what brand/model is the FOH console?")
- **Missing info** — fields the venue packet left blank

The PM sends follow-up questions. This is typically multiple email threads, sometimes phone calls. Department heads (LD, FOH engineer) may advance directly with their venue counterparts on specific technical questions.

The advance sheet is updated with each confirmed piece of information.

### Phase 4: Logistics Advance (2–4 Weeks Out)

Focus shifts to day-of logistics:
- Confirmed load-in time and truck access
- Local crew call time and crew count
- Catering schedule and menu
- Hotel accommodation (if needed)
- Parking for buses and trucks
- Shore power availability

### Phase 5: Final Advance Call (1–2 Weeks Out)

A 30–60 minute phone call between Tour PM and Venue PM. Everything in the advance sheet is confirmed verbally. This is when last-minute issues surface. The PM walks through the day sheet and confirms every line.

After this call, the advance sheet is essentially locked. Changes after this point create problems.

### Phase 6: Day Sheet Distribution (Day Before / Day Of)

The PM finalizes the **day sheet** — a one-page schedule showing every call time, every event, and every relevant contact for the day. Distributed to:
- All tour crew
- Venue PM
- Promoter rep
- Any local department heads

This is the document everyone operates from on show day.

### Phase 7: Show Day

The PM arrives early (often before the crew call). They:
1. Walk the load-in situation before trucks arrive
2. Check in with Venue PM
3. Set up the production office
4. Distribute any printed materials
5. Manage crew calls and breaks (especially union meal penalties)
6. Oversee the stage build
7. Coordinate timing between departments
8. Handle unexpected issues
9. Manage settlement with promoter
10. Oversee load-out

## The Advance Sheet — What It Actually Contains

A real advance sheet is a structured document, typically 2–5 pages, covering every operational detail of a show. The standard format most PMs use:

### Header
- Date, venue name, city/state
- Promoter name and contact
- Deal type (guarantee, door split, etc.)
- Settlement time and contact

### Show Schedule
- Earliest crew access / building opens
- Load-in window (when trucks can begin unloading)
- Crew call time
- Soundcheck window
- Support act soundcheck (if applicable)
- Doors open to audience
- Show start time
- Set length
- Hard curfew (often has financial penalties if exceeded)
- Load-out window
- Building must be clear by

### Venue Contacts
- Production Manager (primary contact)
- General Manager
- Box Office
- Catering contact
- Security manager
- Emergency / after-hours contact

### Stage
- Full stage dimensions (W × D × H)
- Performing area dimensions
- Stage height above audience floor
- Stage surface type (plywood, Masonite, black Marley, etc.)
- Wing space (stage left and stage right)
- Trim / grid height
- Downstage edge to FOH position (throw distance)
- Fly system (counterweight, motorized, none)
- Number of line sets (battens)
- Motor / rigging point count and capacity per point
- Rigging inspection date

### Audio
- FOH console (manufacturer, model)
- Monitor console (manufacturer, model)
- Main PA system (manufacturer, model, configuration)
- Front fills
- Stage monitors (wedge brand/model, how many mixes)
- IEM system (if house provided)
- Wireless systems (brands, frequency coordination)
- Amplifiers / DSP
- Comms system
- Decibel limit (if any)

### Lighting
- Console (manufacturer, model)
- Moving lights inventory (spots, washes, beams — count and model)
- Conventional fixtures
- Follow spots (count, position, operator quality)
- Strobe / blinders
- Haze / fog machines
- Truss configuration and load capacities
- DMX universe count

### Video
- LED wall (size, pixel pitch, processor)
- Cameras (count, positions, operator)
- Video switcher
- Projector / screen (if applicable)

### Power
- Service type (single phase, 3-phase)
- Total service size (amps)
- Voltage
- Available to touring production (how much of the total)
- Panel locations and distance to stage
- Distro connector type (Cam-Lok, 4/0 Lugs, etc.)
- Shore power for buses (amps, location)
- Generator on-site?

### Load-In & Parking
- Dock address (may differ from main venue address)
- Dock height above ground
- Dock door dimensions
- Number of loading bays
- Dock leveler
- Forklift available?
- Pallet jack available?
- Freight elevator (dimensions, weight capacity)
- Distance from dock to stage
- Dead case / empty storage area
- Truck / 53' trailer parking
- Tour bus parking
- Shore power at parking?
- Crew / personal vehicle parking
- Overnight parking allowed?

### Crew & Labor
- Union affiliation (IATSE local, non-union)
- Mandatory house crew required? (how many, which positions)
- Minimum stagehand count
- Meal break requirements (union rules)

### Hospitality
- Dressing rooms (count, capacity, location)
- Showers available?
- Production office location
- Catering room location
- Venue-provided catering (what, when)
- Washer / dryer available?
- WiFi (network, password)
- Ice machine?
- Merchandise location and setup

## The Tech Rider — What the Tour Requires

The tech rider is the tour's document that specifies exactly what they need from every venue. It's the counterpart to the venue's tech packet. The advance process is essentially matching one against the other.

A tech rider typically specifies:
- Minimum stage dimensions (deal-breaker if not met)
- PA system requirements (specific brands preferred, or "equivalent")
- Monitor system requirements
- IEM system requirements
- Lighting rig requirements (often just "suitable rock lighting system")
- Power requirements (amps, voltage, 3-phase vs single)
- Rigging requirements (point count, weight capacity)
- Backline (if venue-provided)
- Minimum crew requirements
- Hospitality requirements (temperature, number of people, specific items)

Tech riders are often written ideally — experienced PMs know which elements are negotiable and which are truly required.

## Venue Types and How Advancing Differs

### Club / Small Theater (Under 1,500 cap)
- Often non-union
- Less formal advance — sometimes just a phone call
- Venue may not have a dedicated PM
- Limited power, minimal rigging
- Day-of-show advance common
- FOH often shares space with merch or bar

### Theater (1,500–3,000 cap)
- Mix of union and non-union
- More structured advance process
- Usually has a dedicated Venue PM
- Fly system likely (may have counterweight fly)
- Balcony and mezzanine seating complicates PA placement
- Good production offices
- Often IATSE jurisdiction

### Amphitheater / Outdoor (3,000–20,000 cap)
- Almost always IATSE union crew
- Full production advance required (2–4 weeks minimum)
- Complex power situation (touring distro required)
- Trucks park at dedicated dock area, distance to stage varies
- Weather contingency planning required
- Shore power for buses often available
- Catering for large crew (50–100+ people)
- Full production office

### Arena (10,000+ cap)
- Always IATSE, large local crew (50–150 people)
- Multi-day build common for large productions
- Separate truck dock from loading entrance
- Complex rigging plots, multiple point loads
- Full audio, lighting, video systems touring
- Settlement is complex, multiple revenue streams
- Dedicated catering facility

### Festival
- Fundamentally different advancing process
- PM advances with festival production team, not a venue
- Shared backline (you may not get your preferred gear)
- Stage changeover time is critical and fixed
- No control over load-in logistics
- Stage plot must fit within changeover window
- Multiple acts, shared crew
- Often more restrictive: no pyro, decibel limits, strict curfew

## Common Failure Modes

These are the situations that cause show day problems. Experienced PMs have seen all of them.

**Outdated venue packet**
The PDF the venue sends is from 3 years ago. They've renovated, changed the PA system, moved the power panels. The advance is based on wrong information. Discovery happens at load-in.

**Unresponsive venue contact**
The Venue PM is hard to reach. Emails go unanswered. Follow-ups required. Critical questions remain open until too late.

**Wrong stage dimensions**
The tour's stage set was designed for 40' wide stages. The venue is 36'. No one caught this in advance. Now the set doesn't fit or needs to be modified on the fly.

**Power shortfall**
The advance said 200A available. At load-in, there's only 100A accessible before the main service. Touring distro can't be set up as planned.

**Missing load-in info**
The advance didn't confirm that 53' trailers can't access the dock. The trucks have to park a block away and everything is hand-trucked. Crew call runs long, load-in is behind schedule.

**Union meal penalty**
Crew has been working 5 hours with no break. The meal window has passed. The tour now owes a meal penalty (typically $10–25/person/violation). This adds up quickly on large crews.

**No venue crew advance**
The tour assumed the venue would have X stagehands. The venue scheduled minimum crew. There aren't enough hands for the load-in. Everything is delayed.

**Curfew violation**
Show runs long. Hard curfew hits. Venue cuts power to the PA. Tour owes a curfew penalty (often $500–1,000/minute or flat fees in the tens of thousands).

**Venue contact wrong**
The person listed as the PM on the tech packet left 6 months ago. The new PM didn't update the document. No one knows who to call with questions.

**Changed specs without notification**
Venue upgraded their PA, replaced the console, or changed power configuration. The packet hasn't been updated. What's in the advance is wrong.

## Tools PMs Use Today

**Email**
The primary communication channel for advancing. Threads become long and disorganized. Attachments get buried. "What was the final confirmed trim height?" requires searching through dozens of emails.

**Google Sheets / Excel**
The advance sheet template. Most experienced PMs have developed their own over years. Copy-paste from venue PDFs into the sheet is a standard workflow. Shared with department heads via link or attachment.

**PDF Reader (Adobe Acrobat, GoodReader)**
Opening and reading venue tech packets. Searching for specific values. Annotating. Often on iPad on show day.

**Google Drive / Dropbox**
Storing and sharing tour documents: riders, advance sheets, stage plots, input lists, signed contracts.

**WhatsApp / iMessage Groups**
Day-of show coordination. Department heads, tour crew, local crew leads. "Where are the empty cases?" "Doors in 20." "FOH console has a failed module." Real-time communication that happens entirely outside any formal system.

**Phone / FaceTime**
Final advance calls, urgent issues, settlement discussions. Some Venue PMs prefer phone to email.

**Touring Central / Band HQ**
Some larger tours or management companies use these platforms, but they are not widely adopted at the PM level.

**Notes App**
Personal shorthand for the PM on show day. Quick lookup of critical info.

## What PMs Actually Care About (In Priority Order)

When a PM looks at a venue's tech specs, they're scanning for these things first, because they're deal-breakers or major constraints:

1. **Stage dimensions** — does the production physically fit?
2. **Power** — is there enough load, in the right format, close enough to stage?
3. **Trim height** — does the lighting rig have clearance?
4. **Rigging capacity** — can the building hold the weight?
5. **Load-in access** — can the trucks get there and dock?
6. **Curfew** — is it hard? What are the penalties?
7. **Union situation** — which local, mandatory crew, meal rules?
8. **PA system** — is it adequate or does the tour need to carry their own?
9. **Dressing rooms** — enough rooms for the artist and crew?
10. **Shore power** — are buses taken care of overnight?

Everything else is important but secondary.

## What "Trusting" an Advance Means

Production managers are professionally paranoid. Their job is to anticipate problems before they happen. This means:

- They verify information they're not sure about
- They add notes like "confirmed 3/15 via phone with Mark" to fields they've verbally verified
- They flag anything that seems off or too good to be true
- When something is missing, they assume the worst case until confirmed otherwise
- They've been burned before by wrong information — they have stories

For a data platform to be trusted:
- PMs need to see **when** the info was last updated
- They need to know **who confirmed it** (venue PM vs AI extraction vs crowdsourced)
- They need to be able to **flag fields as unverified** and track when they get confirmed
- The source of each field matters — "extracted from PDF" is less trusted than "confirmed by venue PM this season"

## The Information Flow on a Real Tour

For a 30-show tour, the PM is managing 30 simultaneous advance relationships at different stages. A typical week mid-tour might look like:

- **Show in 5 weeks**: send initial advance email, request tech packet
- **Show in 3 weeks**: follow up on missing power info, send rider again
- **Show in 10 days**: final advance call, lock day sheet
- **Show tomorrow**: distribute day sheet, confirm crew call times
- **Show today**: load-in, show, load-out, settlement
- **Show last night**: post-show report, flag any issues for next time

Multiply this by the number of active advances, and the organizational overhead is enormous. Most of this happens in email and spreadsheets.

## Terminology Reference

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Advance / Advancing | The process of gathering and confirming show-day operational details before arriving at a venue |
| Advance Sheet | The compiled document of all confirmed show details, distributed to crew |
| Day Sheet | Final show-day schedule with all call times and contacts |
| Tech Rider | Artist's technical requirements document |
| Hospitality Rider | Artist's dressing room and catering requirements |

| Input List | Channel-by-channel audio input list |

| MON | Monitor world — the stage audio mixing position |
| IEM | In-Ear Monitor — wireless earpiece system for performers |
| PA | Public Address — the main speaker system |
| Cam-Lok | A type of high-amperage electrical connector used for touring power distribution |
| Distro | Power distribution unit carried by touring productions |
| Shore Power | Electrical hookup for tour buses while parked at venue (avoids running engines overnight) |
| Dead Cases | Empty equipment cases stored off-stage during show |
| Dock | Loading dock where trucks unload |
| Settlement | Financial reconciliation at end of show between tour and promoter |
| Guarantee | The minimum payment an artist is guaranteed regardless of ticket sales |

| Routing | The geographic sequence of tour dates |
| Tour Bible | The master document containing all show details, contacts, and logistics for the full tour |
| Production Manager | The person managing all technical and logistical aspects of touring production |

| House Left / House Right | From the audience's perspective facing the stage |
| Deck | The stage surface |

| Motor / Chain Hoist | Electric hoist used to fly truss and lighting rigs |
| Dimmer | Device that controls electrical power to lighting fixtures |
| DMX | Digital Multiplex — standard control protocol for lighting fixtures |
| Universe | A single DMX data stream (512 channels) |
| Follow Spot | A manually operated spotlight that follows a performer |
| Haze | Atmospheric effect using fine water or oil particles to make light beams visible |
| Pyro | Pyrotechnics — stage explosions, flames, sparks |

## What a PM Wishes Existed

Based on the pain points described above, an ideal tool would:

1. Give them a venue's confirmed, current specs — not a PDF from 3 years ago
2. Let them see what's changed since last time they played there
3. Pre-populate their advance sheet automatically from those specs
4. Flag conflicts between venue specs and their tech rider without them having to do it manually
5. Show them which fields are still unconfirmed and need follow-up
6. Let department heads access just their relevant section (LD sees lighting, FOH sees audio)
7. Be usable on mobile on show day — fast lookup of key specs in a loud venue
8. Reduce the email back-and-forth by letting the venue update their own info directly
9. Track the history of a venue — what was the stage like last time, what changed
10. Generate a formatted advance sheet / day sheet they can distribute without reformatting
